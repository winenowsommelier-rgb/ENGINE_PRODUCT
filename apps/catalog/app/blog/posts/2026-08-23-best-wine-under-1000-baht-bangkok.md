<div class="page-wrap">

    <div class="section">
      <h2>Where value actually lives at this price</h2>

      <p>
        The under-฿1,000 bracket is enormous. Supermarkets pad it with label-forward bottles
        designed to look premium and drink ordinary. To find genuine quality, you need to know
        which countries punch above their weight at volume pricing — and which ones don't.
      </p>

      <h3>Chile and Argentina</h3>
      <p>
        No contest. Chile's Central Valley and Maipo produce Cabernet Sauvignon at a
        price-to-quality ratio that embarrasses Bordeaux at twice the cost. Argentina's
        Mendoza does the same for Malbec. These aren't consolation wines — they're
        the intended format for these grapes at this price. Concha Y Toro and Argento
        didn't build global reputations by accident.
      </p>

      <h3>Southern France and Southern Italy</h3>
      <p>
        The Languedoc-Roussillon in southern France produces more wine than the rest of France
        combined, and the best producers there know the difference between volume and quality.
        Look for IGP designations from Pays d'Oc — the Languedoc's entry-level tier lets
        winemakers work with excellent fruit at low land costs. Southern Italy (Sicily,
        Puglia, Campania) offers similar logic: warm climate, ancient varieties, no prestige
        premium inflating the price.
      </p>

      <h3>Australia at scale</h3>
      <p>
        Australian producers like Wolf Blass have decades of experience making reliable,
        food-friendly wine at mass-market prices. The Eaglehawk range is the honest version
        of that: it doesn't pretend to be something it isn't. It's clean, consistent, and
        built for the glass, not the cellar.
      </p>

      <h3>Germany's Mosel for whites</h3>
      <p>
        Riesling from the Mosel is the most misunderstood category in this bracket.
        Producers like Dr. Loosen and Nik Weis make wines at the ฿750–800 mark that are
        technically sophisticated — precise acidity, mineral texture, genuine complexity —
        and that pair with Thai food better than almost anything else in the shop.
        If you've been reaching past them, you've been leaving the best value on the shelf.
      </p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<!-- SECTION 2 — What to avoid -->
<div class="page-wrap">
  <div class="col-prose">
    <div class="section">
      <h2>What to avoid at this price</h2>
      <p>
        Knowing where to look matters. So does knowing what to walk past.
      </p>

      <div class="avoid-grid">
        <div class="avoid-card">
          <h4>Over-oaked wines that hide flaws</h4>
          <p>Heavy oak is cheap to add and effective at masking poor-quality fruit. A wine that
          leads with "toasty vanilla" and "creamy oak" at ฿400 is almost certainly using wood
          to compensate, not to complement. Real quality at this price is clean fruit and
          honest structure — not a sawmill in a glass.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="avoid-card">
          <h4>Residual sugar marketed as "smooth"</h4>
          <p>The word "smooth" on a back label is almost always code for sweetness added to
          paper over astringency or thin fruit. Genuine smoothness in a red wine comes from
          ripe tannins and proper winemaking — not grams of sugar per litre making the wine
          drink like a cordial.</p>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="callout">
        <div class="callout-label">Sommelier note</div>
        <p>
          If the back label mentions "perfect for those new to wine" alongside descriptors
          like "soft," "easy-drinking," and "approachable" but gives you zero information
          about origin or variety, put it back. That language is marketing, not winemaking.
        </p>
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>
