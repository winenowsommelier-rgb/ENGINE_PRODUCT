Bangkok's wine bars have made "natural wine" the most fashionable phrase in the city's drinking vocabulary — and one of the most loosely used. Natural wine in Bangkok gets confused with organic wine constantly, including on wine lists that should know better. They are related but genuinely different things: one is a certification about farming, the other a philosophy about winemaking. ## Organic Wine: A Rulebook About Farming

Organic is the precise term of the two. It's a legal certification — EU organic, USDA organic, or an equivalent — and it's primarily about what happens in the vineyard: no synthetic pesticides, herbicides, or fertilizers. In the EU, the rules extend into the cellar too, restricting certain additives and capping sulfites below conventional limits.

What organic certification does *not* dictate is style. An organic wine can be made with cultured yeasts, modern temperature control, filtration — every tool of clean, conventional winemaking. That's why most organic wine tastes completely familiar: it's regular wine, farmed more carefully. If you've enjoyed a crisp Prosecco or a polished Bordeaux Merlot that happened to carry a green leaf logo, you've already drunk organic wine without noticing.

A step further along the same road is **biodynamic** farming — organic plus a holistic, almost philosophical system of soil and vineyard management. You don't need to accept the cosmology to notice that some of the world's most obsessive, terroir-focused producers farm this way.

## Natural Wine: A Philosophy About Everything Else

Natural wine has no legal definition — France's *Vin Méthode Nature* charter, introduced in 2020, is the closest thing to one. In practice it means low intervention at every step: organic (or stricter) farming, wild native yeasts instead of cultured ones, nothing added and nothing removed, little or no filtering, and minimal or zero added sulfites.

![Hands picking grapes at harvest](https://images.pexels.com/photos/10922960/pexels-photo-10922960.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&dpr=2&h=650&w=940)

The result tastes different — sometimes slightly, sometimes radically. Natural wines can be hazy in the glass, brighter and juicier on the palate, with a savory or gently funky edge that polarizes drinkers. When it works, it's some of the most alive, food-friendly wine you can pour — which is exactly why Bangkok's natural wine bars pair it so enthusiastically with Thai food. When it doesn't, it tastes like cider that lost its way. The producer matters more here than in any other category.

The relationship in one line: **almost all natural wine is organic, but very little organic wine is natural.**

## FAQ

### What is the difference between natural wine and organic wine?
Organic wine is a legal certification about farming — grapes grown without synthetic pesticides or fertilizers, with some cellar restrictions in the EU. Natural wine is an uncertified philosophy about winemaking: wild yeasts, nothing added or removed, minimal or no sulfites, little or no filtration. Almost all natural wine is organically farmed, but most organic wine is made in a completely conventional style.

### Does organic wine contain sulfites?
Usually yes. EU organic rules permit added sulfites at lower caps than conventional wine, and US "made with organic grapes" wines allow them too. Only some natural wines are made with zero added sulfites — and no wine is entirely sulfite-free, since fermentation produces small amounts naturally.

### Does natural wine give you less of a hangover?
There's no solid evidence for this popular claim. Hangovers are driven overwhelmingly by alcohol quantity, hydration, and individual tolerance — not sulfites or farming methods. Drink natural wine because you like how it tastes, not as a health strategy.

### What does natural wine taste like?
Expect brighter, juicier fruit, lighter extraction, and often a savory, yeasty, or gently funky edge — sometimes with a slight haze in the glass. Styles range from almost-conventional to radically wild, so the producer matters more than in any other wine category. Low-intervention bottles like Santa Julia's Cuy range sit at the approachable end.
