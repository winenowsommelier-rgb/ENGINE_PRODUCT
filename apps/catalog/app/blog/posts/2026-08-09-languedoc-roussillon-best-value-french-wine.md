<!-- SECTION 1: THE LANDSCAPE -->
<div class="blog-section" id="landscape">
  <div class="blog-container">
    <h2 class="blog-heading">The Landscape: Mediterranean Sun and Ancient Vines</h2>
    <p>Languedoc-Roussillon sweeps from the Rhône Delta in the east to the Spanish border in the west, a crescent of garrigue scrubland, limestone hills, and coastal plains baked by almost relentless Mediterranean sunshine. The Romans planted vines here. So did the Greeks before them. The region's vine age — many plots over 80 years old, some pushing a century — is one of its most underappreciated assets.</p>
    <p>Old vines produce less fruit, but that fruit is concentrated. The roots push deep through clay and limestone into the cool subsoil, giving the wines a mineral tension that pure sunshine alone cannot produce. What you get in the glass is the rare combination of Mediterranean warmth and genuine depth: wines that are ripe and generous but not flat, with structure that lets them age surprisingly well.</p>

    <div class="blog-highlights">
      <div class="blog-highlight-card">
        <div class="blog-highlight-icon">☀️</div>
        <h4>Mediterranean Climate</h4>
        <p>300+ days of sunshine per year. Low rainfall. The Tramontane and Cers winds keep humidity in check and protect against rot — natural disease pressure is minimal.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="blog-highlight-card">
        <div class="blog-highlight-icon">🌱</div>
        <h4>Old Vine Heritage</h4>
        <p>Among the oldest-planted vineyards in France. Grenache, Carignan, and Mourvèdre vines planted before World War II are common, producing small, intensely flavoured yields.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="blog-highlight-card">
        <div class="blog-highlight-icon">🪨</div>
        <h4>Diverse Terroir</h4>
        <p>Limestone, schist, clay, sandy plains, and volcanic soils — sometimes within the same estate. This geological variety is why the region produces such a wide range of styles.</p>
      </div>
    </div>

    <p>The dominant grape varieties tell you everything about the region's character. <strong>Grenache</strong> brings red fruit, spice, and body. <strong>Syrah</strong> adds structure, dark pepper, and that signature northern-Rhône elegance when grown on the region's higher, cooler slopes. <strong>Mourvèdre</strong> — earthy, meaty, sometimes almost savage in youth — contributes backbone and ageing potential. Blended together in the classic GSM style, they produce wines that punch well above their price class.</p>
  </div>
</div>

And <strong>Pézenas</strong> — still under the radar even among Languedoc enthusiasts — is where you find the richest, most structured expressions, particularly from older Mourvèdre vines.</p>

<!-- SECTION 3: IGP PAYS D'OC -->
<div class="blog-section" id="igp">
  <div class="blog-container">
    <h2 class="blog-heading">The IGP Pays d'Oc Advantage</h2>
    <p>Beneath the AOP appellations sits a classification that has quietly changed the French wine landscape: <strong>IGP Pays d'Oc</strong> (Indication Géographique Protégée). Think of it as the creative freedom zone of French wine — where producers are not bound by appellation rules about which grapes they can use or how they must blend them.</p>
    <p>The result? You can find a genuinely excellent single-varietal Chardonnay, Cabernet Sauvignon, Pinot Noir, or Merlot grown in the south of France, at a fraction of what the same grape variety costs from Burgundy or Bordeaux. The sunshine and old-vine concentration are there. The AOP price premium is not.</p>

    <div class="blog-callout">
      <div class="blog-callout-title">🍷 Sommelier's Note</div>
      <p>IGP Pays d'Oc is one of the most useful categories for building a wine list on a budget. The classification allows producers to put the grape variety front and centre on the label — something traditional French AOPs rarely do — which makes them immediately readable for customers more accustomed to New World labelling.</p>
      <p>The quality ceiling has risen dramatically over the past decade. Producers who started in the IGP tier are now winning international medals with single-varietal wines that compete seriously with bottles at twice the price from more famous regions.</p>
    </div>

    <p>For the Bangkok wine buyer, the Pays d'Oc tier is especially practical: the wines are accessible in style, clearly labelled by grape, and hold up well to the full spectrum of Thai cuisine — from light seafood dishes to richly spiced meat preparations. They offer genuine French provenance without demanding that your guests already know their Minervois from their Montpeyroux.</p>
  </div>
</div>

    <h2 class="blog-heading">Gérard Bertrand and the Rise of Languedoc</h2>
    <p>No single name has done more to put Languedoc wine on international restaurant lists than <strong>Gérard Bertrand</strong>. A former rugby player turned winemaker, Bertrand grew up in the region and returned to make it his mission to prove that the south of France could produce world-class wine — not just cheap plonk for summer drinking.</p>
    <p>His approach works on two levels simultaneously. At the premium end, his estate wines from Corbières and Roussillon compete with the best bottles from anywhere in France. But it is his approachable, reliably excellent range of IGP Pays d'Oc and regional Languedoc wines that has changed buying habits at scale — putting Languedoc on wine lists in London, New York, Tokyo, and Bangkok.</p>

    <div class="blog-quote">
      <p>Bertrand's genius was making Languedoc legible to the world: wines that speak clearly, carry their terroir honestly, and never ask more of the drinker than they are ready to give.</p>
    </div>

    <p>His commitment to biodynamic viticulture across his estates has also shifted the conversation around quality. When a major commercial producer moves to biodynamics — not as a marketing exercise but as a genuine farming philosophy — it signals that the region's terroir is worth protecting. It has encouraged smaller producers to follow, quietly raising the quality floor across Languedoc.</p>
    <p>For buyers exploring the region for the first time, Bertrand's wines serve as an excellent entry point: consistent, well-made, and representative of what Languedoc does best at each price tier.</p>

    <h2 class="blog-heading">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
    <div class="blog-faq">

      <div class="blog-faq-item">
        <button class="blog-faq-question">Is Languedoc wine good quality?</button>
        <div class="blog-faq-answer">
          <div class="blog-faq-answer-inner">
            Absolutely — and the quality gap between Languedoc and more famous French regions has narrowed dramatically over the past two decades. The region has some of the oldest vines in France, genuinely diverse terroir (limestone, schist, clay, volcanic soils), and a new generation of serious producers committed to quality over volume. At the AOP level — appellations like Pic Saint-Loup, Faugères, and Pézenas — Languedoc produces wines that compete with the best of the Rhône and Provence. At the IGP Pays d'Oc level, it offers some of the most reliable quality-to-value wines produced anywhere in the world.
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="blog-faq-item">
        <button class="blog-faq-question">What is IGP Pays d'Oc?</button>
        <div class="blog-faq-answer">
          <div class="blog-faq-answer-inner">
            IGP stands for <em>Indication Géographique Protégée</em> — a European classification below AOP (Appellation d'Origine Protégée) that offers producers greater flexibility over which grape varieties they can use and how they label their wines. Pays d'Oc covers the entire Languedoc-Roussillon region. Because IGP rules allow single-varietal labelling (Merlot, Chardonnay, Cabernet Sauvignon, Pinot Noir), these wines are more accessible to international buyers who prefer to choose by grape rather than by appellation. The classification covers over 300 million bottles per year, making it the most important IGP in France — and one of the most commercially significant wine categories in the world.
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="blog-faq-item">
        <button class="blog-faq-question">What grape varieties grow in Languedoc?</button>
        <div class="blog-faq-answer">
          <div class="blog-faq-answer-inner">
            The traditional Languedoc varieties are <strong>Grenache</strong>, <strong>Syrah</strong>, <strong>Mourvèdre</strong>, and <strong>Carignan</strong> for reds and rosés, with <strong>Grenache Blanc</strong>, <strong>Roussanne</strong>, <strong>Marsanne</strong>, and <strong>Viognier</strong> for whites. At the IGP Pays d'Oc level, producers can also grow international varieties including <strong>Cabernet Sauvignon</strong>, <strong>Merlot</strong>, <strong>Chardonnay</strong>, <strong>Sauvignon Blanc</strong>, and <strong>Pinot Noir</strong>. This combination of southern French heritage varieties and international grapes makes Languedoc one of the most versatile wine regions in France — there is a style here for almost every palate and occasion.
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="blog-faq-item">
        <button class="blog-faq-question">What is the best Languedoc wine under ฿1,000?</button>
        <div class="blog-faq-answer">
          <div class="blog-faq-answer-inner">
            Our top recommendation under ฿1,000 is the <strong>Gérard Bertrand Naturalys Syrah</strong> (฿775 on sale) — a biodynamic Syrah with genuine complexity and ageing potential that would cost two or three times as much from the northern Rhône. For everyday drinking, the <strong>Grange Mazan Cabernet Syrah</strong> (฿590) offers the most versatility at the lowest price. And if you want something you can open for guests without explanation — something that will please red wine drinkers and hesitant wine drinkers alike — the <strong>Reserve St Martin Pinot Noir</strong> (฿659) is our safe, reliable pick.
