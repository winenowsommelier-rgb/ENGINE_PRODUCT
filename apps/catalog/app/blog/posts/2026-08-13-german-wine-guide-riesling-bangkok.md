German wine has one of the most complex label systems in the world — and most buyers give up before they understand it. Spätlese, Trockenbeerenauslese, Trocken, Halbtrocken: the labels read like a test you didn't study for. This guide solves that, once and for all.

## The Prädikat System Explained

The **Qualitätswein mit Prädikat** (QmP) system is Germany's classification of wine by ripeness — not sweetness, not quality tier, not price. This is the single most important thing to understand about German wine, and the point where most explanations go wrong.

The Prädikat level tells you how ripe the grapes were when they were picked. Riper grapes contain more natural sugar. But whether that sugar stays in the wine as sweetness, or gets fermented out into alcohol, is a *separate winemaking decision* that the label communicates differently — more on that in the next section.

Work your way up the ladder, from the lightest and most delicate to the rarest and most concentrated:

Kabinett

The lightest category. Grapes picked at normal harvest ripeness. Wines are low in alcohol (often 7–9% ABV), elegant, and refreshing. Often made off-dry.

Ripeness

Spätlese

"Late harvest." Grapes picked at least a week after the main harvest. More concentrated fruit, higher potential alcohol, deeper flavour. Can be dry (*Trocken*) or off-dry.

Ripeness

Auslese

"Selected harvest." Individual bunches selected for exceptional ripeness. Rich, golden, complex. Often retains noticeable sweetness and some noble rot (*Botrytis*) character.

Ripeness

Beerenauslese

Individual berries selected — each one hand-picked for perfect overripeness and Botrytis. Intensely sweet, rich as honey, extremely rare. Cellared for decades.

Ripeness

Trockenbeerenauslese

The pinnacle. "Dry berry selection" — Botrytis-shrivelled individual grapes, almost raisined on the vine. Syrup-thick, otherworldly sweetness and acidity. Often costs more than Sauternes Premiers Crus.

Ripeness

🍷 Sommelier Note

There is also **Eiswein** (ice wine) — grapes frozen on the vine, which concentrates sugars dramatically. It sits alongside Beerenauslese in terms of sweetness intensity, though it is made in a different way.

For everyday drinking, focus on Kabinett and Spätlese — these are the levels where Riesling shines brightest as a food wine.

## Trocken vs. Halbtrocken vs. Off-Dry

Here is where the confusion compounds: **Prädikat tells you ripeness. Trocken (or its absence) tells you sweetness.** They are separate axes.

A bottle can be labelled Spätlese *and* Trocken — meaning the grapes were picked late (Spätlese ripeness), but all the sugar has been fermented to dryness. A Kabinett without any dryness designation is typically off-dry, with a little residual sweetness to balance its high acidity.

| Label Term | Residual Sugar | How It Tastes | Food Match |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trocken** (dry) | < 9 g/L | Bone dry, high acid, mineral, no sweetness | Seafood, white meats, sushi |
| **Halbtrocken** (half-dry) | 9–18 g/L | Off-dry, slight sweetness rounds the acid | Mild Thai dishes, pork, salads |
| **Off-dry** (no label) | ~15–40 g/L | Noticeably fruity-sweet but not a dessert wine | Spicy food, soft cheeses, Thai curries |
| **Feinherb** (informal) | ~12–25 g/L | Between Halbtrocken and off-dry; lively, expressive | Versatile — aperitif, light dishes |

The practical takeaway: if a bottle says **Trocken**, expect it to be dry regardless of its Prädikat level. If it says nothing at all, a Mosel Kabinett or Spätlese is almost always off-dry — that slight sweetness is not a flaw, it is the intended style.

## Key Regions: Mosel, Rheingau, Pfalz

Germany has 13 official wine regions (*Anbaugebiete*), but three stand out as the reference points for Riesling — and each produces a recognisably distinct style.

**Mosel**

Steep slate vineyard slopes above the meandering Mosel river. The slate soil absorbs heat during the day and radiates it back at night, allowing Riesling to ripen slowly and retain piercing acidity. Mosel Rieslings are the most delicate: low alcohol (often 7–9%), high-toned florals (peach, apricot, jasmine), with a defining slate-mineral character. The off-dry style is at its most classical here.

**Rheingau**

A south-facing stretch of the Rhine between Wiesbaden and Rüdesheim. Deeper soils, a slightly warmer mesoclimate, and a longer tradition of producing fuller-bodied Rieslings. Rheingau wines are more structured: richer fruit (peach, citrus peel), firmer tannin feel, higher alcohol, and excellent ageing potential. The Trocken style thrives here. Weingut Robert Weil is a benchmark producer.

**Pfalz**

Germany's warmest wine region, sheltered by the Haardt mountains. Riper, more tropical fruit (mango, pineapple), rounder acidity, and higher alcohol. Pfalz Riesling is the most approachable in style — less austere than Mosel, more generous than Rheingau. The region also excels with Gewürztraminer and Grauburgunder (Pinot Gris).

The hallmark of great Riesling: a pale gold colour, a translucent brilliance, and a nose that leads with fruit and follows with minerals.

## Why Riesling Works Brilliantly in Bangkok

Riesling is arguably the wine best-suited to Thai cuisine — and the case for it in a Bangkok climate is stronger than anywhere else in Southeast Asia.

**Spice Counterpoint**

Residual sugar in off-dry Riesling acts as a buffer against capsaicin heat. The sweetness does not eliminate the spice — it creates an interplay that makes both the food and the wine more interesting.

**Low Alcohol**

A Mosel Kabinett at 7.5% ABV is forgiving in Bangkok's heat. Chilled to 10°C, it is as refreshing as it is complex — something few 14% reds can claim in this climate.

**High Acidity**

Riesling's naturally high acidity cuts through rich, coconut-based curries and cleansing the palate between bites — the same role that lime juice plays in Thai cooking itself.

🍷 The Pairing Formula

**Spicy dishes** (green curry, som tum, pad kra pao) → Off-dry Spätlese or Kabinett from the Mosel. The sweetness absorbs the heat.

**Delicate dishes** (steamed fish, raw oysters, green mango salad) → Dry Riesling (Trocken), Rheingau or Pfalz style. The minerality matches the freshness.

**Rich dishes** (massaman curry, pork belly) → Auslese or fuller Spätlese. The weight and natural sweetness stand up to the richness.

## A Word on Grüner Veltliner

Grüner Veltliner is Austria's flagship white variety, not German — but it is consistently stocked alongside German whites in fine wine retailers and restaurants, and the two are natural companions on a list.

Where Riesling emphasises fruit and floral aromatics, Grüner Veltliner is defined by a savoury, peppery character — specifically, a white pepper note that is almost entirely unique to the variety. It is bone dry, high in acidity, and remarkably food-friendly.

If Riesling is the most intellectual white grape in the world, Grüner Veltliner is the most versatile. It is the white wine a chef would design.

If a wine list has both, try them back-to-back with the same dish: an off-dry Riesling and a dry Grüner Veltliner make the comparison vivid. The acidity is similar; everything else diverges.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is German Riesling always sweet?

No — and this is the most common misconception about German wine. German Riesling spans the full spectrum from bone-dry to intensely sweet. The key is reading the label: a bottle that says **Trocken** is dry; a Kabinett or Spätlese without any dryness designation is typically off-dry (slightly sweet). Auslese and above are usually noticeably sweet, though even at Auslese level, a Trocken version can be made. The Prädikat level tells you about ripeness, not sweetness — look for the word Trocken if you want dry.

### What do Spätlese and Auslese mean?

Both are Prädikat levels — classifications of grape ripeness at harvest. **Spätlese** means "late harvest": grapes picked at least a week after the main harvest, producing a richer, more concentrated wine. **Auslese** means "selected harvest": individual bunches are hand-selected for exceptional ripeness, often with some Botrytis (noble rot). Auslese wines are typically richer and more complex than Spätlese, and are usually (but not always) sweet. The Prädikat scale runs: Kabinett → Spätlese → Auslese → Beerenauslese → Trockenbeerenauslese, in ascending order of ripeness and rarity.

### What does Trocken mean on a German wine label?

**Trocken** means "dry" in German. On a wine label, it indicates that the wine has been fermented until almost all the residual sugar has been converted to alcohol — the legal limit is less than 9 g/L of residual sugar (or up to 12 g/L if total acidity is within 2 g/L). A Trocken Riesling will taste crisp and dry, with no sweetness. **Halbtrocken** (half-dry) allows up to 18 g/L — a perceptible but not pronounced sweetness. If neither term appears on the label, the wine is likely off-dry (particularly at Kabinett and Spätlese level from the Mosel).

### What German wine pairs with Thai food?

Off-dry Mosel Riesling (Kabinett or Spätlese, without Trocken on the label) is the single best pairing for spicy Thai food. The residual sweetness buffers the heat of chilli, the high acidity cuts through rich coconut sauces, and the low alcohol keeps things refreshing in Bangkok's climate. For milder dishes — steamed fish, larb, or green mango salad — a Trocken (dry) Riesling or a Grüner Veltliner from Austria works beautifully. Avoid tannic red wines with Thai food in general: the tannins amplify the heat and fight the complexity of the spicing.
