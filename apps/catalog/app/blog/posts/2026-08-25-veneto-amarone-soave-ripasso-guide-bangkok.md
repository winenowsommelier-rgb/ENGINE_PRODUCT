<div class="blog-section-alt" id="valpolicella">
  <div class="blog-container">
    <h2 class="blog-heading">Valpolicella: The Fresh, Everyday Red</h2>

    <p>Valpolicella — the name comes from Latin meaning "valley of many cellars" — is the base layer of Veneto's red wine pyramid. Made from a blend of indigenous grapes, it is one of Italy's most produced DOC reds, and one of its most misunderstood.</p>

    <p>The blend typically centres on three grapes:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Corvina Veronese</strong> — the dominant grape. Red cherry, sour cherry, fresh acidity. Thin-skinned, high in natural acids. The personality grape.</li>
      <li><strong>Rondinella</strong> — adds colour and body. More resistant to disease, useful for blending structure without weight.</li>
      <li><strong>Molinara</strong> — the light grape. Pale, high acid, increasingly optional under modern DOC rules. Some producers drop it entirely.</li>
    </ul>

    <p>A standard Valpolicella is light in body, high in bright acidity, and built for the table — think grilled meats, pizza, pasta with tomato sauce. The Classico designation matters: wines from the original hillside zone (the comuni of Negrar, Marano, Fumane, Sant'Ambrogio, and San Pietro in Cariano) are measurably more complex and site-specific than the expanded zone wines.</p>

    <div class="blog-callout">
      <div class="blog-callout-title">🍷 Sommelier Tip</div>
      <p>Serve Valpolicella slightly cool — around 16°C. Its bright cherry fruit and high acidity make it one of the most food-versatile Italian reds on the table. It handles tomato-based sauces better than almost any other red wine.</p>
    </div>

    <p>The two Valpolicella bottles in our Bangkok range come from producers who take the Classico zone seriously. Danese is your entry point — honest, fresh, everything Valpolicella should be at a fair price. Speri, a fourth-generation family estate in Fumane, is the benchmark: the kind of wine that makes you reconsider how seriously you took Valpolicella before.</p>

<div class="blog-section" id="ripasso">
  <div class="blog-container">
    <h2 class="blog-heading">Ripasso: The Smart Buyer's Secret Weapon</h2>

    <p>If Valpolicella is the everyday red and Amarone is the special occasion splurge, Ripasso is the wine for everyone who wants something in between — and is smart enough to know it exists.</p>

    <p>The technique is literally in the name. <em>Ripasso</em> means "re-passed." After Amarone has finished fermenting in its large wooden vats, the pressed skins — still rich with residual sugars, colour, and flavour — are set aside. Young Valpolicella wine is then poured over those spent Amarone skins for a second fermentation of 10 to 15 days. The result is a wine that has been run through the Amarone machine without actually being Amarone.</p>

<!-- ===================== SECTION 4: AMARONE ===================== -->
<div class="blog-section" id="amarone">
  <div class="blog-container">
    <h2 class="blog-heading">Amarone: Italy's Most Dramatic Wine</h2>

    <p>No Italian wine is made with more deliberate effort than Amarone della Valpolicella. It begins with the same Corvina, Rondinella, and Molinara grapes as an ordinary Valpolicella — but what happens next belongs to a different world entirely.</p>

    <p>At harvest, only the healthiest, most perfectly formed grape clusters are selected and laid on bamboo racks in open-air drying lofts called <em>fruttai</em>. For the next 90 to 120 days — three to four months — the grapes simply rest and lose water through evaporation. By the time they are pressed, they have lost 30 to 40 percent of their original weight. What remains is an impossibly concentrated must: thick, syrupy with sugar, deep in colour, loaded with flavour compounds that were never detectable in the fresh grape.</p>

    <div class="blog-quote">
      <p>"Amarone is not a wine that was made stronger — it is a wine that became itself through patience."</p>
    </div>

    <p>The word <em>Amarone</em> means "the great bitter one." The name comes from contrast: Veneto also makes <em>Recioto</em> from the same process, but stops fermentation early to leave residual sweetness. When a producer let the fermentation run to completion — fermenting all that sugar dry — the result was bitter by comparison. The name stuck, and so did the style.</p>

    <p>What makes Amarone extraordinary:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Alcohol</strong> — typically 15 to 17 percent, sometimes higher. The concentrated must creates more fermentable sugar, which means more alcohol. This is not a wine you open without intention.</li>
      <li><strong>Flavour depth</strong> — dried fig, stewed plum, dark cherry conserve, espresso, bitter chocolate, leather, tobacco, and often a savoury umami finish that evolves for years in the glass.</li>
      <li><strong>Aging potential</strong> — serious Amarone from a top producer and a good vintage can age 15 to 25 years, sometimes longer. Entry-level Amarone from a co-op needs 5 to 8 years minimum.</li>
      <li><strong>Oak aging</strong> — DOCG rules require a minimum of two years in oak for standard Amarone and four years for Riserva. Most serious producers use large Slavonian oak barrels (<em>botti</em>) rather than new French oak, preserving fruit over wood.</li>
    </ul>

    <p>Masi's "Bonacosta" Classico is one of the best introductions to the Veneto at a Bangkok-friendly price — a Valpolicella Classico from one of the region's historic producers that shows you the base material before you invest in Amarone. For Amarone itself, the Bangkok market typically starts around ฿2,500 and rises steeply from there. If you find Masi Costasera, Speri Valpolicella Classico Amarone, or Bertani at a reasonable price, these are the names to trust.</p>

  <div class="blog-container">
    <h2 class="blog-heading">Soave: The Underrated White Nobody Talks About</h2>

    <p>Ask most wine drinkers in Bangkok to name an Italian white, and you will hear Pinot Grigio. Ask a sommelier, and there is a reasonable chance they say Soave — particularly Soave Classico from the volcanic hills east of Verona.</p>

    <p>Soave is made primarily from Garganega, an ancient grape unique to this zone. The best vineyards sit on basalt — volcanic rock — which gives the wines a distinctive mineral salinity and a texture you cannot get from alluvial soils. Think of it as the geological equivalent of Alsace for Riesling, or Chablis for Chardonnay: the soil is doing something irreplaceable, and the wines reflect it unmistakably.</p>

    <p>What Soave Classico tastes like at its best:</p>
    <ul>
      <li>White blossom, peach, lemon zest on the nose</li>
      <li>Almond, white pepper, and a saline mineral quality on the palate</li>
      <li>Medium body, lively acidity, a long and slightly bitter finish — the signature of Garganega</li>
      <li>At its finest (think Pieropan, Anselmi, Inama), a complexity that rivals good white Burgundy at a quarter of the price</li>
    </ul>

    <p>The phrase "the Riesling of Italy" is an oversimplification, but it captures something true: Soave Classico is a food-versatile, terroir-expressive white that rewards attention and ages far better than its affordable price suggests. It is remarkable with seafood, Thai salads, grilled chicken, and soft cheeses.</p>

    <p>Speri's Soave Classico DOC is the companion piece to their Valpolicella — same family philosophy, opposite side of the appellation map. Try them side by side if you can. The contrast between the white and the red tells you more about Veneto than any tasting note.</p>

  <div class="blog-container">
    <h2 class="blog-heading">Prosecco: A Quick Word on Veneto's Famous Fizz</h2>

    <p>Veneto's most exported wine is not Amarone, Ripasso, or Soave. It is Prosecco — the sparkling wine made from Glera grapes in the hills between Treviso and Conegliano-Valdobbiadene, in the northern reaches of the region. Italy exports over 600 million bottles of Prosecco a year, and Veneto produces the majority of it.</p>

    <p>Prosecco's bubbles are made using the Charmat method — secondary fermentation in pressurised steel tanks rather than in the bottle — which preserves fresh fruit aromatics and keeps production costs low. The resulting wine is lighter, fruitier, and less yeasty than Champagne, designed for immediate enjoyment rather than cellaring.</p>

    <p>Prosecco Superiore DOCG from Conegliano-Valdobbiadene or Asolo is a step above the standard DOC: hillside vineyards, lower yields, more texture. The single-vineyard Rive wines represent the appellation's finest expression. But for most occasions, well-made Prosecco DOC is cheerful, affordable, and exactly what it sets out to be.</p>

<!-- ===================== FAQ ===================== -->
<div class="blog-section-alt" id="faq">
  <div class="blog-container">
    <h2 class="blog-heading">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

    <div class="blog-faq">

      <div class="blog-faq-item">
        <button class="blog-faq-question">What is Amarone wine?</button>
        <div class="blog-faq-answer">
          <div class="blog-faq-answer-inner">
            Amarone della Valpolicella is a dry red wine from the Veneto region in northeastern Italy, made using the <em>appassimento</em> method — a technique where freshly harvested Corvina, Rondinella, and Molinara grapes are dried on bamboo racks for 90 to 120 days before pressing. This drying process concentrates the sugars, colours, and flavour compounds in the grape, resulting in a wine of extraordinary richness. Amarone is typically full-bodied, high in alcohol (15–17%), and loaded with flavours of dried fig, dark cherry, chocolate, espresso, and leather. It is one of Italy's few DOCG-classified reds and carries a corresponding price point.
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="blog-faq-item">
        <button class="blog-faq-question">What is the difference between Amarone and Ripasso?</button>
        <div class="blog-faq-answer">
          <div class="blog-faq-answer-inner">
            Both Amarone and Ripasso come from the Valpolicella zone and share the same base grapes, but they are made very differently. Amarone is a fully independent wine made by drying the grapes (appassimento) and fermenting the concentrated must to complete dryness. Ripasso is made by taking a finished Valpolicella wine and re-fermenting it over the leftover grape skins from Amarone production — a technique called "re-passing." The result picks up some of Amarone's richness, dried-fruit character, and structure without the full weight or cost of Amarone. Ripasso sits between Valpolicella and Amarone in terms of body, complexity, and price — which is exactly what makes it such good value.
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="blog-faq-item">
        <button class="blog-faq-question">Is Soave a good white wine?</button>
        <div class="blog-faq-answer">
          <div class="blog-faq-answer-inner">
            Soave Classico is an excellent white wine that is significantly underrated outside of Italy and the sommelier community. Made primarily from Garganega — a grape unique to this zone — on volcanic basalt soils east of Verona, good Soave has a distinctive mineral salinity, lively acidity, and flavours of white peach, lemon zest, almond, and white blossom. The best examples from the hillside Classico zone can age 5 to 8 years and develop real complexity. If you enjoy Pinot Grigio but want more texture and character, or if you find Chardonnay too heavy, Soave Classico is almost certainly the Italian white you have been waiting to discover.
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="blog-faq-item">
        <button class="blog-faq-question">How long can you age Amarone?</button>
        <div class="blog-faq-answer">
          <div class="blog-faq-answer-inner">
            Serious Amarone from a top producer — Bertani, Dal Forno Romano, Quintarelli, Masi, Speri — can age for 15 to 25 years from a good vintage, and exceptional bottles have been documented lasting 40 years or more. The high alcohol acts as a natural preservative, and the concentrated tannins from dried grape skins need years to integrate and soften. Entry-level Amarone from a cooperative or a lighter vintage is best consumed within 8 to 12 years. Amarone Riserva — which must age at least 4 years in oak before release — is made for the longest cellaring. If you are buying Amarone to drink tonight, look for bottles from 2012 or earlier to ensure the wine has had time to open up.
          </div>
