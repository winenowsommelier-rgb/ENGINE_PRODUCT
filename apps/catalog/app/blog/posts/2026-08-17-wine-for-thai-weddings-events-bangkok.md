  <div class="blog-container">
    <h2 class="blog-heading">Budget Tiers: What Your Money Buys</h2>
    <p>Bangkok's import wine market gives you genuine quality at every price point — if you know where to look. Here is how to think about each tier when you are buying by the case.</p>

    <h3 class="blog-subheading">฿400–600 per bottle — The Volume Tier</h3>
    <p>This is the workhorse of event wine. At this price, you are looking at well-made commercial wines from Chile, Australia, Spain, and South Africa — all countries that have mastered the art of consistent, drinkable everyday wine. A good bottle in this bracket is approachable, fruity, not aggressively tannic, and capable of holding its own with Thai food's bold flavors without disappearing into the heat. Buy most of your volume here.</p>

    <h3 class="blog-subheading">฿600–1,000 per bottle — The Mid-Tier Step-Up</h3>
    <p>Guests who pay attention will notice the difference. Mid-tier wines have more structure, a cleaner finish, and often come from better-known regions or producers with a stronger reputation. This tier is ideal for the main dinner service at a wedding, or for corporate events where the wine reflects on your brand. You do not need to go here on every bottle — blending mid-tier at dinner tables and volume-tier at cocktail stations is a smart play.</p>

    <h3 class="blog-subheading">฿1,000–1,500 per bottle — Premium Table &amp; Gift</h3>
    <p>Reserve this tier for the top table at a wedding, VIP guests at a corporate dinner, or as take-home gifts. Wines here carry real prestige — names and regions that mean something to guests who know wine — without crossing into territory that feels extravagant for a mass-catered event. A Grand Reserva from Chile or a quality Tuscan red at this price point will be genuinely appreciated.</p>

<div class="blog-section" id="crowd-pleaser">
  <div class="blog-container">
    <h2 class="blog-heading">Crowd-Pleaser Principles</h2>
    <p>Event wine has one job: make every guest comfortable. The wines you love to drink alone — a grippy Barolo, an aggressively oaky Chardonnay, a bone-dry Muscadet — can be brilliant in the right context but alienating at a Thai wedding table of twenty people who mostly drink beer or spirits. Keep these principles in mind:</p>

    <ul>
      <li><strong>Avoid tannic monsters.</strong> High tannin (Nebbiolo, Cab Franc, young Bordeaux) clashes with spicy and fish-sauce-forward Thai food and leaves a drying sensation that puts casual drinkers off. Reach for smoother, fruit-forward reds: Shiraz, Merlot, Malbec, Grenache blends, or softer Italian reds.</li>
      <li><strong>Skip heavily oaked whites.</strong> A wine that smells like a sawmill will confuse guests expecting something light and refreshing. Choose crisp, clean whites: Sauvignon Blanc, unoaked Chardonnay, Pinot Grigio, or dry Riesling.</li>
      <li><strong>Fruit-forward wins the room.</strong> Berry-driven reds and citrus-driven whites read as approachable and pleasant to drinkers at every experience level. They also pair well with the wide variety of dishes served at Thai banquets.</li>
      <li><strong>Think about finish length.</strong> Long, complex finishes are wasted on guests chatting across a loud table. Save complexity for the few bottles you open at the top table.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="blog-section-alt" id="bangkok-heat">
  <div class="blog-container">
    <h2 class="blog-heading">Bangkok Heat &amp; Humidity: A Real Factor</h2>
    <p>Wine service in Bangkok — especially for outdoor gardens, rooftop terraces, or hotel lobby receptions where air conditioning is imperfect — is not the same as service in a climate-controlled European cellar. Two rules apply that most event planners ignore until it is too late:</p>

    <div class="blog-highlights">
      <div class="blog-highlight-card">
        <div class="blog-highlight-icon">🧊</div>
        <h4>Chill the Reds</h4>
        <p>Serve red wine at 16–18°C rather than "room temperature." Bangkok's room temperature is 28°C. A red wine served that warm loses its fruit character and tastes flat and alcoholic. Fifteen minutes in a bucket of ice water before service is all it takes.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="blog-highlight-card">
        <div class="blog-highlight-icon">🪣</div>
        <h4>Ice Buckets for Whites</h4>
        <p>White and sparkling wine warms in minutes in Bangkok humidity. Request one ice bucket per two tables minimum. Brief the venue or your event manager before the day — this is often overlooked in standard setup packages.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="blog-highlight-card">
        <div class="blog-highlight-icon">🍾</div>
        <h4>Sparkling as a Welcome Drink</h4>
        <p>A chilled sparkling wine or Prosecco as a welcome drink doubles as a crowd-pleaser and a heat relief. It sets the tone of the event without requiring any wine knowledge from guests to appreciate.</p>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="blog-callout">
      <div class="blog-callout-title">🌡️ Quick Tip</div>
      <p>If you are running an outdoor night wedding in Bangkok between April and October, budget for more ice than you think you need. Wine and cold water consumption both spike in high humidity. Contact your supplier about pre-chilled delivery options for large orders.</p>
    </div>
  </div>

<div class="blog-section" id="faq">
  <div class="blog-container">
    <h2 class="blog-heading">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

    <div class="blog-faq">

      <div class="blog-faq-item">
        <button class="blog-faq-question">How many bottles of wine for 50 people?</button>
        <div class="blog-faq-answer">
          <div class="blog-faq-answer-inner">
            For a 2-hour event with 50 guests, order 50 bottles — one per person. For a 3-hour or longer event such as a wedding reception with a full dinner, order 75 bottles (1.5 per person). Add a 10% buffer on top of that figure to cover spillage, over-pours, and guests who arrive early. For a 3-hour event for 50 people, a safe order is therefore around 82–85 bottles. Split roughly 70% red and 30% white: approximately 57–60 red and 25–26 white.
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="blog-faq-item">
        <button class="blog-faq-question">Should I buy red or white wine for a Thai wedding?</button>
        <div class="blog-faq-answer">
          <div class="blog-faq-answer-inner">
            Both, but lean red. The 70/30 red-to-white split is the standard recommendation for Thai wedding receptions because red wine pairs more naturally with the meat dishes, grilled proteins, and rich sauces common in Thai banquet menus. However, for a daytime or early-evening garden reception during the hotter months — March through June — you may want to push toward 50/50 or even slightly more white and sparkling to account for the heat. Always include at least a few bottles of dry sparkling wine for toasts.
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="blog-faq-item">
        <button class="blog-faq-question">Can I get wine delivered to an event venue in Bangkok?</button>
        <div class="blog-faq-answer">
          <div class="blog-faq-answer-inner">
            Yes. Wine-Now offers delivery to most event venues, hotels, and private residences across Bangkok. For large orders, we recommend placing your order at least 5–7 days in advance to allow for logistics coordination, especially for weddings requiring 50 or more cases. Some hotel venues have corkage policies — check with the venue first and let us know when you order. You can reach our events team directly on LINE @wine-now for a custom quote and delivery scheduling.
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="blog-faq-item">
        <button class="blog-faq-question">What is a safe crowd-pleaser wine?</button>
        <div class="blog-faq-answer">
          <div class="blog-faq-answer-inner">
            A safe crowd-pleaser wine has three characteristics: it is fruit-forward (immediately appealing, not austere), low to moderate in tannin (so it does not dry out the palate of guests who are not regular wine drinkers), and neither too sweet nor too aggressively dry. For reds, think Chilean Cabernet Sauvignon, Australian Shiraz, or a soft Italian red like Negroamaro. For whites, think New Zealand or Chilean Sauvignon Blanc, or an unoaked Pinot Grigio. Avoid anything described as "earthy," "tannic," "barnyard," "minerally," or "austere" on an event wine label — those are wines for connoisseurs, not celebrations.
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>
