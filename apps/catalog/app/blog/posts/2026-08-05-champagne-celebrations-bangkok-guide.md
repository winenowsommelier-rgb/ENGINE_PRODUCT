<div class="blog-section" id="nv-vs-vintage">
  <div class="blog-container">
    <h2 class="blog-heading">NV vs. Vintage Champagne: What the Labels Actually Mean</h2>
    <p>The two most common letters you'll encounter on a Champagne label are <strong>NV</strong> — Non-Vintage. They're not a downgrade. They're a house's signature: a blend of wines from multiple harvests, assembled to taste consistent year after year. When you buy a Champagne Alain Navarre Cuvée Tradition Brut or an Ernest Rapeneau NV, you're buying a winemaker's crafted house style, not a single season's luck.</p>
    <p><strong>Vintage Champagne</strong>, by contrast, is declared only in exceptional years when the harvest is good enough to stand alone. The label carries a year — 2018, 2015, 2012 — and the wine in the bottle came exclusively from that harvest. Vintage bottles are aged longer (typically five years or more) before release, which is why they cost more and taste more complex: deeper toast, evolved fruit, a creamier texture.</p>

    <div class="blog-highlights">
      <div class="blog-highlight-card">
        <div class="blog-highlight-icon">🍾</div>
        <h4>Non-Vintage (NV)</h4>
        <p>Blended across multiple years for consistent house style. Ready to drink now. The majority of all Champagne produced — and where the best everyday value lives.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="blog-highlight-card">
        <div class="blog-highlight-icon">📅</div>
        <h4>Vintage</h4>
        <p>A single exceptional harvest, aged longer. More complexity, higher price, limited production. A gift for someone who will appreciate the specific year and the story behind it.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="blog-highlight-card">
        <div class="blog-highlight-icon">🎯</div>
        <h4>The Bangkok Verdict</h4>
        <p>For most celebrations — birthday dinners, corporate toasts, New Year — a well-chosen NV from a quality house outperforms an average Vintage at twice the price.</p>
      </div>

  <div class="blog-container">
    <h2 class="blog-heading">The Bangkok Context: Prices &amp; What to Expect</h2>
    <p>Bangkok is not a cheap city for Champagne. Import duties, excise tax, and VAT stack up quickly, and the final retail price typically lands <strong>40–60% above London or Paris retail equivalents</strong>. A bottle you'd pay £12 for at a Parisian cave will often hit ฿1,300–1,500 in Bangkok. That's a meaningful premium, but it's worth putting in perspective.</p>
    <p>Compared to <strong>Singapore</strong>, Bangkok is still notably more competitive — Singapore's higher GST, import fees, and distribution costs routinely add another 15–25% on top. For wine tourists and expatriates doing a regional comparison, Bangkok consistently comes out ahead.</p>

<div class="blog-section-alt" id="faq">
  <div class="blog-container">
    <h2 class="blog-heading">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
    <div class="blog-faq">

      <div class="blog-faq-item">
        <button class="blog-faq-question">Is Moët &amp; Chandon actually good quality?</button>
        <div class="blog-faq-answer">
          <div class="blog-faq-answer-inner">
            Yes — Moët &amp; Chandon is a technically well-made Champagne, produced by one of the oldest and largest houses in the region. What it is not is a hidden gem or a connoisseur's secret. You're paying partly for the brand recognition, which is exactly what makes it an excellent corporate gift or status-signal purchase. If pure liquid quality per baht is your goal, a grower Champagne at the same price point will usually deliver more complexity. If the name on the label is part of the occasion, Moët earns its place.
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="blog-faq-item">
        <button class="blog-faq-question">What is NV Champagne?</button>
        <div class="blog-faq-answer">
          <div class="blog-faq-answer-inner">
            NV stands for Non-Vintage. It means the wine is a blend of base wines from multiple harvests, rather than a single year's production. This is actually the dominant style in Champagne — it allows the winemaker to maintain a consistent house character year after year, compensating for the variable climate of northeastern France. NV Champagnes are not inferior to Vintage; they're simply made for a different purpose: reliability, approachability, and immediate enjoyment.
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="blog-faq-item">
        <button class="blog-faq-question">How much does good Champagne cost in Bangkok?</button>
        <div class="blog-faq-answer">
          <div class="blog-faq-answer-inner">
            Genuine Champagne (AOC-protected, made in the Champagne region of France) starts at around ฿1,299 in Bangkok for a quality NV from a smaller house. The mid-range sweet spot runs from ฿1,500 to ฿2,500 — this is where you'll find the best quality-to-price ratio. Big-name houses (Veuve Clicquot, Moët, Pol Roger) typically start at ฿2,500–3,500 for a full bottle. Bangkok prices run roughly 40–60% above equivalent bottles in Paris or London, primarily due to import duty and excise tax — but they remain competitive against Singapore and Hong Kong.
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="blog-faq-item">
        <button class="blog-faq-question">How long does Champagne keep after opening?</button>
        <div class="blog-faq-answer">
          <div class="blog-faq-answer-inner">
            An opened bottle of Champagne, resealed with a wine stopper or a proper Champagne stopper, will hold reasonable fizz for 24–36 hours in the fridge. A dedicated Champagne stopper (the hinged kind that clamps onto the lip) works significantly better than plastic stoppers — it maintains pressure rather than just covering the opening. Beyond 48 hours, the bubbles are largely gone, though the wine itself remains drinkable as a still wine for another day or two. If you have half a bottle left, the best course is usually to finish it that evening.
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>
