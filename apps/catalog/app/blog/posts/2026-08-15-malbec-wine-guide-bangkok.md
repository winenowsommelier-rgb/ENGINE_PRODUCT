    <h2>From Bordeaux Footnote to Argentina's National Wine</h2>
    <hr class="section-rule">
    <p>Malbec originated in the Cahors region of south-west France — a tough, tannic, deeply coloured grape that the great Bordeaux châteaux used sparingly to add structure to their blends. It was never the star. Phylloxera, the vine pest that devastated European vineyards in the late 19th century, nearly finished it off entirely on the continent.</p>
    <p>But in 1853, a French agronomist named Michel Pouget carried Malbec cuttings across the Atlantic to Argentina. The vines arrived in Mendoza, a high-altitude desert region on the eastern slopes of the Andes, and something remarkable happened: the grape transformed. The harsh sun, the thin air, the dramatic temperature swings between day and night — every stress that would have troubled a French vine became a quality signal in Argentina.</p>
    <p>By the early 2000s, Argentine producers had realised they were sitting on one of the wine world's great secrets. International sommeliers noticed. Restaurants in New York, London, and eventually Bangkok followed. Within fifty years, a grape that France had nearly abandoned had become one of the most recognised red wine varieties in the world — and Argentina had built an entire national wine identity around it.</p>

<!-- SECTION 2: ALTITUDE -->
<div class="section-alt" id="altitude">
  <div class="wrap">
    <h2>The Mendoza Altitude Story</h2>
    <hr class="section-rule">
    <p>To understand why Argentine Malbec tastes the way it does, you need to understand altitude. Mendoza's vineyards are planted between 700 and 1,500 metres above sea level — in some sub-zones, even higher. At that elevation, three things happen that you simply cannot replicate at sea level.</p>

    <h3>Intense UV Radiation</h3>
    <p>Thin mountain air filters less of the sun's ultraviolet radiation. The vines respond by producing more anthocyanins — the pigments that give Malbec its famously deep, almost inky purple-ruby colour. More UV stress also means thicker grape skins, which translate directly into more tannin, more flavour compounds, and more of those distinctive violet and dark fruit aromas that make Malbec so recognisable.</p>

    <h3>Diurnal Temperature Swings</h3>
    <p>Mendoza days are hot and sunny — temperatures regularly top 30°C in summer. But the nights, fed by cold air descending from the Andes snowfields, can drop to 10–12°C. This dramatic diurnal swing — often 20°C or more between day and night — is a winemaker's gift. Heat during the day ripens sugar and builds body. Cold nights preserve the grape's natural acidity and slow aromatic development, trapping those perfumed violet and red-fruit notes inside the berry rather than letting them blow off.</p>
    <p>The result is a wine that can be simultaneously rich and fresh — full-bodied enough to stand up to a good steak, but with enough natural acidity to keep things lively in the glass.</p>

    <div class="callout">
      <p class="callout-title">Sommelier Note</p>
      <p>When comparing two Malbecs at similar price points, look at the elevation on the label. A wine from 1,200 m in the Uco Valley will typically show more freshness and finesse than one from the lower-altitude plains. It's not always better — sometimes you want that rich, plush style — but altitude tells you a great deal about what's in the glass before you even open it.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<!-- SECTION 4: FLAVOUR -->
<div class="section-alt" id="flavour">
  <div class="wrap">
    <h2>Flavour Profile: What Malbec Actually Tastes Like</h2>
    <hr class="section-rule">
    <p>Malbec has a flavour profile that is almost uniquely crowd-pleasing — rich enough to feel indulgent, but with enough structure to be interesting.</p>

    <div class="img-wrap">
      <img src="https://images.pexels.com/photos/1407846/pexels-photo-1407846.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&dpr=2&h=650&w=940"
           alt="A glass of deep ruby Malbec held against warm light">
      <p class="img-caption">The deep ruby-purple colour of a well-made Malbec — a direct result of thick skins grown under intense Andean UV radiation.</p>
    </div>

    <h3>Primary Aromas</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Dark plum and blackberry</strong> — the signature fruit character, especially at lower altitudes</li>
      <li><strong>Violets</strong> — a distinctive floral lift that sets Malbec apart from Cabernet Sauvignon</li>
      <li><strong>Black cherry</strong> — more prominent in higher-altitude examples</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Secondary Aromas (oak-influenced)</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Cocoa and dark chocolate</strong> — from barrel ageing, integrates beautifully with the fruit</li>
      <li><strong>Vanilla and cedar</strong> — common in oak-aged expressions</li>
      <li><strong>Tobacco leaf</strong> — appears in more structured, aged bottles</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>On the Palate</h3>
    <p>Malbec's most crowd-pleasing trait is its tannin structure. Compared to Cabernet Sauvignon, the tannins are softer and rounder — sometimes described as "velvety." This means you can enjoy a good Malbec young, without needing to cellar it for years. The finish is typically warm and persistent, with dark fruit and a gentle chocolate note lingering.</p>

    <div class="quote-block">
      <p>Malbec is the red wine that converts white wine drinkers. The tannins never scratch, the fruit is always generous, and there is always something interesting going on behind the obvious pleasure.</p>
    </div>

  <div class="wrap">
    <h2>Price-Per-Quality: Why Malbec Wins in Bangkok</h2>
    <hr class="section-rule">
    <p>Here is a blunt fact that sommeliers talk about quietly but wine drinkers deserve to know openly: for a full-bodied red wine, Malbec offers some of the best price-per-quality of any variety available in Bangkok right now.</p>
    <p>Why? Argentina's production costs remain lower than Bordeaux, Napa, or even much of Italy. The category is not artificially inflated by trophy wine speculation the way some Burgundy and Bordeaux appellations are. And the quality-to-price ceiling has been rising steadily as Argentine producers invest in better winemaking and single-vineyard expressions.</p>
    <p>What this means practically: at ฿600–฿700, you can drink a genuinely good Malbec from a real Mendoza producer. At ฿1,000–฿1,200, you are getting wine that competes with bottles costing two or three times as much from other regions. That gap is why Malbec has earned a place on so many Bangkok lists — the restaurants know it delivers, and so do their customers.</p>

<!-- SECTION 7: FOOD PAIRING -->
<div class="section" id="food">
  <div class="wrap">
    <h2>Malbec with Bangkok Food — What Works and What Doesn't</h2>
    <hr class="section-rule">
    <p>One of Malbec's great strengths is its versatility with food. The soft tannins and ripe fruit are forgiving partners — but there are still smart choices and a few things to avoid.</p>

    <h3>What Works Well</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Grilled beef</strong> — the classic pairing, and for good reason. Malbec's dark fruit and gentle tannins are built for char and protein. A good ribeye or wagyu at any Bangkok steakhouse works beautifully.</li>
      <li><strong>Pork neck (คอหมู) — grilled or roasted</strong> — the fattiness of pork neck softens Malbec's tannins and brings out its fruit. Thai-style charcoal-grilled pork neck is a genuine pleasure with a glass of Argento or Trapiche.</li>
      <li><strong>Aged cheese</strong> — a hard, aged cheese like parmesan or manchego amplifies Malbec's savoury, chocolatey notes. Great for a wine-and-cheese evening in.</li>
      <li><strong>Lamb</strong> — an underrated pairing. The grape's herbal and earthy undertones complement the richness of lamb beautifully.</li>
      <li><strong>Mushroom-based dishes</strong> — the earthy, umami quality of mushrooms aligns naturally with Malbec's darker fruit and cocoa character.</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>What Doesn't Work</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Very spicy Thai food</strong> — high alcohol and heat in the same mouthful amplifies both. The wine tastes harsh, the food tastes hotter. If you want wine with a spicy meal, reach for a sweeter Riesling or a dry rosé instead.</li>
      <li><strong>Delicate seafood</strong> — Malbec's body overwhelms light fish. The wine simply bulldozes everything subtle on the plate.</li>
      <li><strong>Very sweet sauces</strong> — teriyaki or hoisin-glazed dishes with pronounced sweetness make Malbec taste flat and less fruity.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
